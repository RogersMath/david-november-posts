Mon Nov 2

Most improper surgical dressing payments trace to paperwork

Medicare reviewers look closely at surgical dressing claims. For the 2024 period, CMS reports a 57.6% improper payment rate for surgical dressings, a projected $177 million.

Nearly all of the errors came down to records. CMS attributes 48.6% of the improper payments to no documentation and another 43.8% to insufficient documentation.

The order, the wound measurements, and the dressing change schedule all need to be in the record before the claim goes out.

Source: CMS, Surgical Dressings compliance tips, 2026.

Wed Nov 4

Soft silicone or hydropolymer on stage II pressure ulcers

A randomized trial compared a soft silicone bordered dressing with a hydropolymer dressing on stage II pressure ulcers. It enrolled 38 nursing home residents aged 65 or older: 18 on silicone, 20 on hydropolymer.

Healing was close. Eight of 18 wounds healed with silicone and 10 of 20 with hydropolymer. Reports of tissue damage at removal numbered 2 with silicone and 32 with hydropolymer.

The trial was small and open-label, and pain was not measured. The authors said the findings need confirming in other controlled studies.

Source: Meaume et al., Ostomy Wound Management, 2003.

Fri Nov 6

Registered, cleared, exempt: three different FDA words

FDA uses specific words for device status, and they mean different things.

Cleared: FDA issued a 510(k) letter finding the device substantially equivalent to a legally marketed device.

Exempt: most Class I and some Class II devices do not need a 510(k) but still must meet other FDA requirements.

Registered: the company is listed with FDA. FDA says a registration entry does not mean a device or facility is approved, cleared, or authorized.

Knowing which word applies helps when comparing suppliers.

Source: FDA, Premarket Notification 510(k) and Device Registration and Listing guidance.

Which surgical dressings need PDAC verification?

PDAC, the Medicare contractor that reviews product coding, keeps a list of items that need coding verification.

Among surgical dressings, only two entries appear: collagen dressings (A6021 to A6024) and one gradient compression wrap (A6545). For those codes, a claim is denied if the product is not on PDAC's Product Classification List.

Foam, alginate, and specialty absorptive codes are not on that required list, though their codes still follow Medicare's coding guidelines.

Source: DME PDAC, Items Requiring Coding Verification Reviews.

A new dressing means a new order

Medicare ties surgical dressings to a written order. A new order is needed when a new dressing is added or the quantity increases, and every three months for each dressing in use.

The order includes the patient's name or MBI, the order date, an item description, the quantity if it applies, and the practitioner's name or NPI and signature.

So a supplier or product change is a paperwork event as well as a clinical one.

Source: CGS Administrators, Surgical dressings: information for prescribers.

Skin tears: pick the dressing, then plan the removal

The 2025 ISTAP skin tear recommendations favor silicone dressings where available. They report that silicone improves skin tear healing compared with non-silicone, non-stick dressings and is gentler than acrylic adhesives.

Removal gets attention too: use an adhesive remover, lift slowly, and mark an arrow on the dressing showing the direction to peel.

A 5 to 7 day change interval can be appropriate, unless heavy drainage causes leakage or maceration, which is skin softened by constant moisture.

Source: Ousey et al., Wounds International, July 2025.

Mon Nov 16

Scoring exudate so a team describes it the same way

Exudate is the fluid a wound makes. Too much or too little can delay healing.

The 2019 World Union of Wound Healing Societies consensus says exudate should be assessed as part of a structured wound assessment. Its expert group favored the Falanga Wound Exudate Score, which has three levels, and advised staying consistent in the method used.

The document also notes that no widely accepted exudate measurement tool exists yet.

Source: Schultz et al., WUWHS Consensus Document, Wounds International, 2019.

Wed Nov 18

Matching the dressing group to the drainage

Medicare's surgical dressing policy ties each dressing group to how much a wound drains.

Foam is for full-thickness wounds with moderate to heavy drainage. Alginate and other fiber gelling dressings are for moderately to highly draining wounds and are not reasonable on dry wounds or wounds covered with eschar, which is dry, dead tissue. Hydrogels are for full-thickness wounds with minimal or no drainage.

Drainage level is the first thing to check on the chart.

Source: CMS, LCD L33831, Surgical Dressings.

Layered dressings need matching change schedules

When dressings are layered, Medicare looks at how often each one changes. The schedule follows the dressing that touches the wound, and the dressing over it has to fit that schedule.

CMS gives an example: a weekly secondary dressing over a daily primary dressing is denied.

CGS adds a supply limit. No more than a month's supply may be provided at one time unless the record supports more.

Source: CMS, Surgical Dressings compliance tips; CGS Administrators, prescriber guidance.

Mon Nov 23

One shared dressing list across a health system

A Johns Hopkins team described a health system with acute, home, long-term, and outpatient care that had too many advanced wound care products and unclear vendors.

A committee of wound specialists, purchasing staff, and value analysts, led by two certified wound care nurses, grouped products by performance and fit across settings. Six similar or rarely used products came out of one category.

The abstract projected savings above $50,000 a year, a forecast, not a result. Home care, outpatient, long-term care, and four hospitals agreed on one shared list.

Source: Owen et al., WOCN Society conference abstract, June 2012.

Why one training session is not enough

An English community program cut a list of about 700 dressings to 70. Monthly education sessions alone did not improve practice: attendance was low and use of the list stayed below the 75% target.

The author recommends ongoing access to guidance and required training for new staff. The program later reported list use above 85% across about 66 surgeries, the UK term for general practices.

The paper was prepared with support from a dressing manufacturer, which is noted in the document.

Source: Knight, Wounds UK, 2010;6(2).

Mon Nov 30

Where a dressing is billed depends on the setting

The same dressing can be billed in different ways depending on where the patient is.

During a Part A skilled nursing stay, only items on the SNF excluded list may be billed to the DME MAC, the Medicare contractor for durable medical equipment claims. After the Part A stay ends, surgical dressings are on the covered list. During a 60-day home health episode, dressings fall under home health consolidated billing and cannot be billed separately.

Checking the setting first can prevent a denial.

Source: Noridian, Consolidated billing, DME MAC Jurisdiction A, 2026.

Vet 1

Hydrocolloid and hydrogel on canine skin wounds

A 2003 study compared three treatments on full-thickness skin wounds in 12 dogs: a hydrocolloid dressing, a hydrogel dressing, and saline as the comparison. Each dog had six 2 by 2 cm wounds on the trunk.

For the first week the groups did not differ significantly. By day 21, hydrogel wounds had significantly more total healing than the others (P < 0.02), and more epithelialization, the growth of new skin over the wound, at day 14 and day 21.

Each dog served as its own control, and the group was small.

Source: Kwon et al., J Vet Clin, 2003;20(3):294-301.

Vet 2

Why the tissue under the skin matters in dogs and cats

Researchers made paired wounds on either side of the midline in dogs and cats, then removed the layer of tissue under the skin on only one side.

Sutured wounds healed the same either way. Open wounds that healed on their own did not: removing that layer reduced blood flow, granulation (new tissue), contraction, epithelialization, and overall healing. Granulation and contraction were delayed more in cats than in dogs.

The authors concluded that extensive removal of this tissue should be avoided, especially in cats.

Source: Bohling et al., Vet Surg, 2006;35:3-14.

Vet 3

Serious bandage injuries: what one case series found

A case series described 11 animals, 9 dogs and 2 cats, with serious injuries from bandages that cut off blood flow.

Nine needed surgical debridement, 5 needed skin grafts, and 3 had toe amputations. Two had a leg amputated and 2 died. Long term, only 4 of the 11 had a good result.

A veterinary news summary lists bandages applied too tightly, thin padding over pressure points, and swelling under the bandage as causes, and says most serious problems appear in the first 24 to 48 hours.

Source: Anderson and White, Vet Surg, 2000;29(6):488-498.

Vet 4

Collagen and collagen-alginate sheets on dog wounds

A study of 12 dogs with full-thickness skin wounds, including bite wounds, traumatic injuries, and chronic wounds, tested two sterile bovine-based sheets: a collagen-alginate sheet and a collagen-only sheet.

Dressings were reapplied every 48 hours for two more applications, then changed weekly. A punch biopsy on day 28 was used to look at healing under the microscope.

Both dressings were well tolerated with no adverse reactions, and both appeared to support healing. With 12 dogs, the study was small.

Source: Vishnu et al., J Vet Anim Sci, 2016;47(2):36-38.

Vet 5

A moist dressing for skin flaps in cats

A small study in 5 cats used a moist dressing impregnated with an antibiotic to protect distant skin flaps, tissue moved from the chest or belly to cover limb wounds. Dressings were changed on days 3, 6, 9, and 12.

The flap was cut from its donor site at 7.2 days on average. Pain response scores fell from 2.0 on day 3 to 1.0 by day 12, where lower is better. The authors concluded the moist dressing helps stabilize the flap.

There was no control group, and the scores were subjective.

Source: Erwin et al., Vet World, 2021;14(3):734-738.

Vet 6

Infected dog wounds and an alginate hydrogel

A 2024 clinical trial in Mexico City treated 56 dogs with full-thickness, infected cavity wounds that had not responded to a full course of antibiotics. The treatment was a sodium alginate hydrogel containing the antibiotic enrofloxacin.

All dogs healed. Mean time to full recovery was 6.75 days for the mildest wounds, 13.76 days for the middle group, and 24.47 days for the most severe.

The comparison group came from historical case records.

Source: Gutierrez et al., Acta Vet Brno, 2024;93(3):305-312.

Plastics 1

Six donor-site dressings in one randomized trial

A randomized trial in 14 centres compared six dressings on skin graft donor sites: alginate, film, gauze, hydrocolloid, hydrofibre, and silicone. It randomized 289 adults.

Hydrocolloid healed donor sites 7 days faster than the others, a median of 16 days versus 23 (P < 0.001). Infection occurred in 18% of patients with gauze versus 7.6% with the other dressings. Overall pain was low.

The authors recommended discontinuing gauze.

Source: Brölmann et al., Br J Surg, 2013;100(5):619-627.

Plastics 2

Silicone tape on abdominal scars after breast reconstruction

A randomized trial tested silicone tape on abdominal scars after breast reconstruction with the patient's own tissue. It enrolled 41 patients. Tape went on one side of each scar 2 weeks after surgery and was worn 24 hours a day for three months. The other side was the comparison.

Blinded surgeons scored the scars with the observer scale. The taped side scored better at 3 months (p = 0.0005) and 6 months (p = 0.0025).

Patient-reported scores and 12-month results were not given in the abstract.

Source: Burke et al., AAPS annual meeting abstract, 2025.

Silicone sheets and oxygen therapy for scars after surgery

A 2025 prospective study compared silicone sheets with hyperbaric oxygen therapy for scars after surgery. Thirty-three patients were analyzed, 15 with silicone sheets and 18 with oxygen therapy, and scars were scored at weeks 4, 8, and 12.

The paper reports no statistically significant differences between the groups at any time point. The authors concluded both are effective scar management options.

It was a small study with no untreated control group.

Source: Chen et al., Int J Med Sci, 2025;22(5):1167-1175.

Plastics 4

A dressing for high-risk breast cancer surgery

A retrospective study at one hospital looked at 42 patients having breast cancer surgery who were at high risk of wound complications. A combined hydrofiber-hydrocolloid dressing was compared with a traditional dressing.

Risk factors were balanced between groups. Wound complications at one week were lower with the advanced dressing (P = 0.015), and it was easier for staff to remove (P = 0.026).

The authors note complication rates after oncologic breast surgery can reach 33%. The study was small and single-center.

Source: Nicotera et al., Plast Reconstr Surg Glob Open, 2021;9(11):e3911.

Plastics 5

What a hydrogel dressing meta-analysis found

A 2019 meta-analysis pooled 43 studies comparing hydrogel dressings with other dressings.

Hydrogel shortened healing time for second-degree burns, diabetic foot ulcers, traumatic skin injuries, and radiation skin injuries, and relieved pain in second-degree burns, traumatic skin injuries, and wounds from laser treatment. For surgical wounds, healing time did not differ significantly, though cure rates were higher.

The authors concluded hydrogel dressings speed healing across several wound types and show no severe adverse reactions.

Source: Zhang et al., Front Bioeng Biotechnol, 2019;7.

Plastics 6

What a Cochrane review says about silicone gel sheeting

A Cochrane review pooled 20 trials with 873 participants on silicone gel sheeting for hypertrophic and keloid scars.

For prevention in people prone to scarring, sheeting lowered the rate of hypertrophic scarring compared with no treatment (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.98). For treatment, it reduced scar thickness and improved scar color.

The authors described the evidence as weak and the studies as highly susceptible to bias.

Source: O'Brien and Jones, Cochrane Database Syst Rev, 2013;(9):CD003826.
